# Model card: price-elasticity-regressor

## Model details

- **Type:** per-product log-log constant-elasticity demand regression
  (`ln Q = a + e * ln P`), estimated with **ridge and lasso implemented from
  scratch** - ridge closed-form via `scipy.linalg.solve` plus a
  gradient-descent variant a test shows converges to it; lasso via coordinate
  descent with soft-thresholding. Estimates feed the **Lerner optimal-markup
  rule** `P* = MC * |e| / (|e| - 1)`, guarded to return the price cap when
  |e| <= 1 (no interior optimum). Implementation:
  [`mllab/price_elasticity_regressor/model.py`](../../mllab/price_elasticity_regressor/model.py).
- **Estimation config:** per product, ridge closed form with l2 = 1e-3 on
  [ln P] (naive) or [ln P, Z] (controlled), intercept unpenalised - 2 or 3
  coefficients per product, 60 products. **Hierarchical shrinkage** then
  pulls each product's controlled estimate toward its segment's pooled mean
  with James-Stein-style precision weighting, so thin products borrow
  strength.
- **One improvement over the base method:** the shrinkage for thin segments,
  plus an explicit, *demonstrated* endogeneity caveat (below) rather than a
  footnote.

## Intended use

- Demonstrate two things pricing work gets wrong: (1) naive OLS on
  observational price/quantity data is **biased** when price responds to
  demand conditions, and (2) estimation accuracy is not the objective -
  *decision quality* (profit at the recommended price) is. Portfolio exhibit
  and template for margin analytics.
- **Out of scope:** setting real prices from observational data with this
  code as-is - the bias correction works here only because the confounder Z
  is observable by construction; and any market where cross-price effects,
  competitor response, or dynamic/promotional pricing matter (the model is
  static, single-product, own-price only).

## Training data

- Synthetic, generated by `mllab.synth.make_transactions(seed=11)`: 60
  products in 6 segments, 50-300 transactions each (~25% of products
  deliberately thin at 50-70 transactions). True elasticities are drawn per
  segment from U(-2.6, -1.3) with per-product noise (sd 0.15) - all elastic
  by construction.
- **Endogeneity is baked in on purpose:** each transaction has a latent
  demand shock Z that raises quantity (coefficient U(0.6, 1.2)) *and* raises
  the posted price (gamma = U(0.4, 0.8)) - mimicking a merchant who prices
  into demand. Z is stored in the data so the "controlled" regression can use
  it. True marginal cost and demand intercept are recorded for the profit
  simulation.
- Deterministic under the seed (tests enforce this).

## Evaluation

- **Metrics: elasticity bias and RMSE against the known true elasticities,
  R^2, and two decision metrics** - mean profit *regret* vs the oracle price
  (profit evaluated on the *true* demand curve at the recommended price) and
  profit uplift vs a cost-plus policy (MC x 1.5). Why: only synthetic data
  makes true-elasticity RMSE measurable at all, and the portfolio's KPI rule
  is that a pricing model must be judged by the money its recommendations
  leave on the table, not by fit - a small elasticity error near |e| = 1
  produces a huge price error, so RMSE alone misleads.
- **Measured (seed 11, printed by `python -m mllab.price_elasticity_regressor`):**
  naive OLS elasticity bias **+1.517** (RMSE 1.582) - i.e. demand looks far
  less elastic than it is; adding the Z control collapses the bias to
  **+0.027** (RMSE 0.160, R^2 0.872); shrinkage tightens RMSE 0.160 ->
  **0.129**. Decision quality: mean profit regret **0.89%** vs the oracle,
  uplift **+18.72%** vs cost-plus.

## Limitations and failure modes

- **The endogeneity fix is a demonstration, not a method:** the confounder Z
  is observable *because the generator saves it*. On real data the demand
  shock is latent; recovering the true elasticity requires an instrument or
  an observable cost-shifter, and without one the +1.5 bias this repo
  demonstrates cannot even be detected, let alone removed. Naively pricing
  from the biased estimate would systematically over-price (demand looks
  inelastic).
- **The |e| <= 1 guard is honest but useless as advice:** for inelastic
  estimates the Lerner rule has no interior optimum and the code returns the
  price cap. All synthetic products are elastic by construction, so this
  branch is never stress-tested by the measured results.
- **Constant elasticity and static demand:** log-log assumes one elasticity
  at every price level, no cross-product substitution, no competitor
  reaction, no intertemporal effects (stock-up behaviour after a price cut).
  The 0.89% regret is measured inside a world where the functional form is
  exactly right.
- **Shrinkage assumes segments are exchangeable:** pulling a thin product
  toward its segment mean helps when the segment is homogeneous and hurts
  when a product genuinely deviates; with 6 segments of 10 products the
  between-product variance estimate (tau^2) is itself noisy.

## Ethical and deployment considerations

Elasticity-driven pricing is where analytics touches customers' wallets
directly. The headline failure mode is not technical but distributional:
segment-level price optimisation can shade into charging customer groups
"what they will bear", and in B2B settings that can mean small buyers with
fewer alternatives face the steepest markups; in some jurisdictions
segment-differentiated pricing also has legal constraints. The honest
technical finding of this model - that observational estimates are badly
biased without a valid control - doubles as an ethical one, because
over-priced products harm customers before the P&L notices. A deployment
should validate elasticities with deliberate, randomised price tests rather
than observational fits, cap recommended price moves per cycle, review
segment definitions for fairness and legality, and keep a pricing analyst
accountable for every recommendation the Lerner rule produces.
