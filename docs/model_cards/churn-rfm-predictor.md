# Model card: churn-rfm-predictor

## Model details

- **Type:** L2-penalised logistic regression **written from scratch in
  numpy** (sigmoid, weighted BCE, analytic gradient
  `X^T(sigma(Xw+b) - y)/n + 2*lambda*w`, full-batch gradient descent; a
  finite-difference test pins the gradient), followed by **Platt calibration**
  (Platt 1999) fit on held-out logits. RFM framing per Bult & Wansbeek 1995.
  Implementation:
  [`mllab/churn_rfm_predictor/model.py`](../../mllab/churn_rfm_predictor/model.py).
- **Inputs:** 6 standardised features - recency_days, frequency, monetary,
  tenure_days, an engineered declining-order-frequency slope (freq_slope), and
  avg_basket. The model has 7 parameters (6 weights + bias); the Platt
  calibrator adds 2 more.
- **Training config:** lr 0.5, 3,000 iterations, l2 = 0.01, seed 7;
  class weight on positives = (1 - pos_rate)/pos_rate computed on the fit
  split; standardisation statistics fit on training data only and reused for
  the test set. The training cohorts are split 80/20 internally so Platt
  calibration is fit on logits the classifier never trained on.
- **One improvement over the base method:** the freq_slope feature, a
  time-based cohort split, and calibrating *after* class-weighting (weighting
  deliberately distorts probabilities to help ranking; Platt repairs them).

## Intended use

- Demonstrate that a retention workflow needs *calibrated probabilities*, not
  just a ranking - because a retention budget ("call everyone above 40% churn
  risk") multiplies probability errors into money. Portfolio exhibit and
  template for B2B at-risk scoring.
- **Out of scope:** decisions about individual real customers (the model has
  only ever seen synthetic ones); any use where a churn score changes the
  *terms* a customer is offered (pricing, credit, service priority) rather
  than triggering an outreach; and reuse of the calibrated probabilities
  after the base rate has drifted from the training cohorts'.

## Training data

- Synthetic, generated by `mllab.synth.make_customers(seed=7)`: 4,000
  customers with gamma/Poisson-distributed RFM features, a signup
  `cohort_month` (0-23), and a churn label drawn from a logistic latent with
  known coefficients (recency +0.9, frequency -0.5, monetary -0.3, tenure
  -0.6, freq_slope -1.1, plus a recency x slope interaction +0.8), with the
  intercept calibrated so overall churn is ~15%.
- **Split:** time-based - train on cohorts 0-15 (n = 2,660), test on cohorts
  16-23 (n = 1,340, test churn rate 0.142). No customer appears on both
  sides.
- **Assumptions baked in:** churn is a single binary event with a clean
  label, features are stationary across cohorts, and the true relationship is
  logistic with one interaction - so a logistic model is *correctly
  specified* here, which flatters it relative to real data. Label noise is
  irreducible (Bernoulli draws), so a perfect score is impossible by design.
- Deterministic under the seed (tests enforce this).

## Evaluation

- **Metrics: PR-AUC (primary), plus ROC-AUC, Brier score, ECE (10-bin) and a
  reliability curve.** Why: at ~15% prevalence, accuracy and ROC-AUC both
  reward the easy negatives; PR-AUC scores the minority class the budget
  actually targets. Brier and ECE are there because this model's stated
  purpose is *probabilities* - a ranking metric alone cannot detect that the
  class-weighted model's raw outputs are miscalibrated.
- **Measured (seed 7, printed by `python -m mllab.churn_rfm_predictor`):**
  PR-AUC **0.653** vs recency-only baseline 0.361 and prevalence baseline
  0.150; ROC-AUC 0.871; Brier 0.135 -> **0.078** and ECE 0.197 -> **0.021**
  after Platt calibration. The calibration step is the headline: before it,
  predicted probabilities overstate churn risk badly (ECE 0.197); after it
  they can be read at face value on this test split.

## Limitations and failure modes

- **Calibration drift is the model's Achilles heel:** Platt parameters are
  fit once, on cohorts 0-15. Churn base rates move with macro conditions,
  pricing changes, and seasonality; a stale calibrator quietly turns "40%
  risk" into an over- or under-statement while the *ranking* still looks
  fine, so the drift is invisible to anyone watching only PR-AUC.
  Recalibration on the newest complete cohort must be part of every scoring
  cycle.
- **The Platt fit rests on ~532 samples (~80 positives):** the 20% inner
  calibration split of 2,660 training rows is small, so the two calibration
  parameters carry real variance; the measured ECE 0.021 should be read as
  "calibration works", not as a precise guarantee.
- **Correct specification is a synthetic luxury:** the generator's latent
  really is logistic in these features, so linearity costs little here. Real
  churn has non-linearities and censoring (a "churned" B2B customer may just
  be between quarterly orders); the model has no concept of an observation
  window ending.
- **cohort_month is a signup-time split, not a behaviour-time split:** it
  prevents the same customer leaking across the split, but real leakage risks
  (features computed after the label window opens, late-arriving order
  corrections) are not exercised by this data.

## Ethical and deployment considerations

Churn scores change how customers are treated, and both error directions have
victims: a false "will churn" can trigger discounts that train customers to
threaten leaving (or, in worse designs, *reduced* investment in a customer
written off as lost), while a false "safe" means a genuinely unhappy customer
gets no attention. Because the inputs are behavioural (recency, frequency,
spend), small or seasonal customers are structurally more likely to look
"at risk", so treatment driven by these scores can systematically favour
large accounts. A deployment should use scores to prioritise *positive*
outreach rather than to withhold service, keep the score's inputs explainable
to the account owner (this 7-parameter model at least makes that easy), audit
outcomes by customer segment, and - because the probabilities feed budget
decisions - treat the recalibration cadence in the previous section as an
ethical requirement, not just a technical one.
